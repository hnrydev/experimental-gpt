WORLD FIELDS PRIMER
Purpose: give a compact, organized map of major domains of knowledge and how they connect.
This is not a full encyclopedia. It is structured text so a language model can learn repeated patterns:
definitions, relations between fields, and careful language about uncertainty and evidence.

=== METADATA ===
Knowledge is split into fields for human organization. Reality itself is one connected system.
Fields share methods: observation, measurement, formal reasoning, modeling, and criticism.
Good claims separate: (1) definitions, (2) assumptions, (3) evidence, (4) conclusions, (5) limits.

=== FORMAL FOUNDATIONS ===
Logic: study of valid inference. Propositional logic uses true and false and connectives.
Predicate logic adds objects, relations, and quantifiers for all and there exists.
A proof is a sequence of steps from axioms or premises to a theorem by rules.
Mathematics: study of structure, quantity, change, and space, built from careful definitions.
Arithmetic: numbers and operations. Algebra: symbols, equations, and structures like groups.
Calculus: limits, derivatives, integrals. Linear algebra: vectors, matrices, linear maps.
Probability: measure of uncertainty; random variables; expectations; common distributions.
Statistics: learn from data with models; estimation, testing, intervals, and prediction.
Information theory: entropy, codes, and limits of compression and communication.
Computation: algorithms, complexity, and what machines can compute in time and memory.

=== PHYSICAL SCIENCES ===
Physics: matter, energy, space, and time. Classical mechanics: forces, energy, momentum.
Thermodynamics: heat, work, entropy, and equilibrium. Electromagnetism: fields, light, circuits.
Relativity: spacetime, light speed, gravity as geometry in general relativity.
Quantum theory: states, amplitudes, measurement, uncertainty, entanglement at small scales.
Chemistry: atoms, bonds, reactions, phases, and energy changes. Organic chemistry: carbon-based molecules.
Materials science: properties of solids, defects, and design of new materials.
Earth science: geology, weather, climate, oceans, and long timescales of the planet.
Astronomy: stars, galaxies, cosmology, and the large-scale history of the universe.

=== LIFE SCIENCES ===
Biology: life, heredity, evolution, cells, and ecosystems. Genetics: DNA, variation, and expression.
Evolution: natural selection, drift, speciation, and the tree of life. Ecology: populations, communities, energy flow.
Neuroscience: neurons, brain circuits, perception, and behavior mechanisms.
Medicine: diagnosis, treatment, and evidence from trials; public health at population scale.
Epidemiology: spread of disease, modeling, interventions, and uncertainty in real data.

=== SOCIAL AND HUMAN SCIENCES ===
Psychology: mind, behavior, learning, memory, emotion, and measurement of human performance.
Cognitive science: mind as computation plus neuroscience, language, and development.
Sociology: groups, institutions, networks, and social patterns. Anthropology: culture, language, and comparison across societies.
Economics: allocation, incentives, markets, prices, trade, and macro fluctuations.
Political science: power, institutions, voting, and policy analysis. History: evidence, narrative, and interpretation of past change.
Linguistics: sound, grammar, meaning, and language change over time. Education: how people learn in practice and at scale.
Law: rules, rights, and institutions; not identical to moral philosophy but interacts with it.

=== HUMANITIES AND NORMATIVE FIELDS ===
Philosophy: clear questions about existence, mind, value, and knowledge, with arguments.
Ethics: what we ought to do, rights, justice, and tradeoffs. Metaethics: nature of moral claims.
Aesthetics: art, beauty, and interpretation, often without single true answers in the math sense.
Literary studies: texts, genres, and criticism with attention to context and style.
Theology and religious studies: studied as human traditions, texts, and practices in academic mode, distinct from any one faith claim in this primer.

=== ENGINEERING AND APPLIED FIELDS ===
Engineering: design under constraints, safety, testing, and iteration.
Computer science: systems, software, hardware, and human use of machines.
Data science: pipelines, features, models, and careful evaluation in messy settings.
Control theory: feedback and stability in systems from robots to the grid.

=== EPISTEMOLOGY AND PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENCE ===
Epistemology asks what knowledge is, how it is justified, and what counts as good reasons.
Skepticism: demands standards before accepting strong claims. Empiricism: emphasis on evidence from experience.
Rationalism: emphasis on reason and structure beyond raw sense data alone.
Falsification: a scientific theory should be testable; predictions that fail force change.
Paradigms: shared frameworks, puzzles, and shifts in how communities solve problems in science.
Underdetermination: many theories can fit the same data; extra criteria matter: simplicity, scope, and fruitfulness.
Bayesian thinking: update beliefs with evidence; priors and likelihoods, not just one yes or no.
Causation is not the same as correlation. Interventions, mechanisms, and counterfactuals are central in careful work.

=== UNCERTAINTY, BIAS, AND ERROR ===
All measurements have noise. Models are simplifications. Overfitting: fitting noise, not pattern.
Selection bias, confounding, and nonresponse can break naive conclusions from data.
Reproducibility: independent checks, open methods, and honest reporting of limits.
Adversarial examples show ML systems can be brittle. Calibration: probabilities that match long-run frequency.

=== SYSTEMS, COMPLEXITY, AND EMERGENCE ===
Many small parts can produce large scale behavior not obvious from a single part alone.
Feedbacks, thresholds, and networks appear in climate, markets, biology, and infrastructure.
Game theory: strategic interaction with payoffs, equilibria, and cooperation or conflict.
Chaos: deterministic rules with sensitive dependence, limiting long term prediction in practice.

=== COGNITION AND RATIONALITY ===
Human reason uses heuristics that are fast and often good, but can fail in systematic ways.
Metacognition: thinking about what we know, monitoring confidence, and revising.
Educated reasoning combines formal tools with domain knowledge and checks of one s own process.

=== HOW FIELDS FEED INTO WORLD MODELS ===
A world model in the loose sense is internal structure for predictions, not a claim of perfection.
It combines: stable facts, dynamics, and uncertainty, with a way to update when new information arrives.
Science is the best public method we have to reduce guesswork about repeatable patterns, but it is slow, partial, and corrected over time.
Ethics, law, and policy must act before certainty arrives; that makes explicit values and process essential.

=== LANGUAGE AND LINGUISTICS (BRIEF) ===
Phonetics studies speech sounds; phonology studies how they pattern in a language. Morphology studies word
structure; syntax studies how words build sentences; semantics studies meaning; pragmatics studies use in
context. Sociolinguistics studies variation by group and setting; psycholinguistics studies processing in
the mind; historical linguistics studies change over time. Writing systems map language to visible marks; they
are not languages themselves. Translation is not a look-up; languages carve meaning differently.

=== ARTS, MEDIA, AND CULTURE ===
Literature, film, music, and games encode stories and style; criticism interprets with attention to form
and context. Media literacy means asking who made a message, what evidence it uses, and what is left out.
Copyright and fair use (where they exist) balance creators, users, and follow-on creation; details vary
by country. Museums, archives, and open collections preserve and share material culture; provenance
matters for history and equity.

=== GEOGRAPHY, TIME, AND HUMAN ORGANIZATION ===
Maps are models with projections that distort. Latitude and longitude locate points; time zones and calendar
rules are social conventions, though Earth rotation is physical. A country is a political entity, not always
the same as a single culture or language. Oceans, climate zones, and biomes shape where people can live
and how. Urbanization, migration, and remittances are large-scale human patterns, each with data and story.

=== MORE LIFE SCIENCE CONNECTIONS ===
Cell biology, biochemistry, and molecular biology connect chemistry to living processes. Developmental
biology studies how bodies form; immunology studies defense and when it misfires. Biostatistics and study
design matter because biology is noisy. Public health is population-scale: sanitation, food safety, and
vaccination programs, not only individual care.

=== MORE PHYSICAL AND EARTH SCIENCE ===
Geophysics, meteorology, and hydrology use measurement and models for earth systems. Plate tectonics explains
earthquakes, volcanism, and long-term land shape. Nuclear physics and chemistry underpin energy and
medicine; they also need careful safety culture. Electrochemistry and materials matter for batteries and
for the energy transition, alongside policy and cost curves.

=== KNOWLEDGE ORGANIZATION AND TOOLS ===
Encyclopedias, textbooks, and databases are curated differently; the web is vast and uneven. Peer review
and editorial process differ by field; preprint servers post fast without implying full review. Citation
graphs and systematic reviews help summarize evidence, but garbage in still yields garbage out. Versioned
data and open methods improve trust for computational work.
