GRAMMAR AND CLARITY CORPUS
Purpose: flood the training data with well-formed modern English. Most lines here
are model answers to the question, "What should fluent text look and sound like?"
A small fraction is labeled "Avoid" followed by a correction, so the model can learn
contrast, but the weight of tokens should favor clean text. This file is original
synthetic training text, not a full style manual. Retrain after any corpus change.

=== SENTENCE CORE: SUBJECT AND VERB ===
Every clear sentence in English needs a main clause most of the time. The subject
tells the reader who or what acts or exists. The verb tells what happens or what
is true. The report explains the result. The cats sleep on the rug. The wind has
weakened since morning. In questions, the subject and auxiliary can invert: Is the
door locked? Do the children know the time? In commands, the subject "you" is
understood: Open the window. Add salt slowly.

=== AGREEMENT: PERSON AND NUMBER ===
The file is here. The files are here. She runs every week. They run on Sundays.
The team is ready; the team members are ready. Neither the manager nor the
engineers are satisfied yet. A box of parts sits on the shelf, because "box" is
the head noun. A number of issues remain open. The number of errors is small this
week. None of the tests pass. None of the results is conclusive in this phrasing
style; both "pass" and "is" are seen in formal usage, so prefer consistency in one
document.

=== PRONOUNS: CLEAR ANTECEDENTS ===
Maria called Lee because she needed an answer. This sentence is ambiguous. Better:
Maria called Lee because Maria needed an answer, or: Maria called Lee, who could
answer the question. The laptop is slow, and it might need a new drive. The laptop
and the phone are old, and they will be replaced. When a person applies, he or she
submits a form, or, in a modern inclusive style, when a person applies, that
person submits a form, or: applicants submit a form.

=== TENSE: CONSISTENCY IN A PARAGRAPH ===
Yesterday the team met. They reviewed the list. The lead engineer presented the
plan, and the others asked questions. Now the same story in the present, as
habit: Each week the team meets. They review the list. The lead engineer
presents the plan, and the others ask questions. Mixing past and present in one
narrative confuses a reader unless you signal a shift on purpose, as in: We built
the first version, and the new model addresses the same users with fewer steps.

=== MODIFIERS: PLACE WORDS SO THEY MODIFY THE RIGHT THING ===
I saw a dog in the car that was barking. This placement ties "barking" to the
car, which is funny, not the goal. Better: I saw a barking dog in the car, or: In
the car, I saw a dog that was barking. The editor returned the manuscript on
Tuesday, not "returned the manuscript in a good mood on Tuesday" unless the mood
stays where you intend.

=== COORDINATION: PARALLEL FORM ===
She likes to write, to edit, and to review. She likes writing, editing, and
reviewing. She likes to write, editing, and to review is not parallel, so the
list feels wrong. The job is to plan the test, to run the test, and to record the
results. The job is not to plan the test, run the test, and recording, unless
you are very careful with gerunds and to-forms in one series.

=== LINKING IDEAS: CONJUNCTIONS AND ADVERBS ===
The result is small, but it is real. The result is small; however, it is real. The
result is real; the effect is local. A semicolon can join two closely related
independent clauses. A comma after a short introductory phrase is often helpful:
After lunch, the group resumed work. In summer, the office runs a lighter
schedule, not: In summer the office runs, without a pause when a brief comma helps.

=== AVOID: RUN-ONS AND COMMA SPLICES (LABELED) ===
Avoid: The server came up the test passed we left. Use: The server came up, and
the test passed, so we left. Or use three short sentences. Avoid: It was late, we
went home. Use: It was late, so we went home, or: It was late. We went home. The
word "so" and the period are both honest solutions.

=== GOOD SHORT PARAGRAPHS: DESCRIPTIVE ===
The small kitchen faces east. Light enters through a single window above the sink.
A wooden table stands against the long wall, and two chairs sit beneath a shelf
of plain plates. Nothing in the room is new, but the floor is clean, and the air
smells faintly of coffee from the morning. This is enough detail for a reader to
imagine a space without a list of every object.

A winter storm arrived overnight. By morning, snow had covered the walks, and
branches sagged over the road. A plow passed once, and then a second time, as
the flurries continued. Schools announced a delay, not a full closure, which was
a relief to parents who still had deadlines at work. The day remained gray until
the wind shifted after noon.

The software starts with a blank project screen. The user can import a file or
begin from a template. Each choice opens a different panel, but the main toolbar
stays the same, so a beginner does not have to learn two interfaces at once. A
status line at the foot of the window shows the current file name and a simple
health check for the last save. Error messages are short, with a code in brackets
if the user must search for help. This tone is how professional tools respect the
user's time.

=== GOOD PARAGRAPHS: EXPLANATORY ===
A confidence interval in statistics is not a statement that the true value has a
certain probability of living inside the interval. Instead, the interval is
constructed from a process that, if you could repeat the study, would capture the
true value at a long-run rate. One realized interval, after the data are seen, is
fixed, so casual language can misread it. A careful report states the design and
gives a graph, not a slogan about certainty.

A battery in a phone or laptop is a package of electrochemical cells, managed by
a circuit that balances charging speed against heat. Every full cycle ages the
chemistry a little, but keeping the state of charge in the middle range, when the
device and software allow, can lower stress. Cold temperatures reduce available
current for a time; heat is worse for long-term life. A user who wants maximum
longevity can avoid leaving the device at a hundred percent and hot in a
sunlit car, which is a simple, practical plan.

=== GOOD PARAGRAPHS: NARRATIVE (PAST) ===
She left the house before sunrise and carried a thermos in her coat pocket. The
bus was empty for the first three stops, so she had time to reread the email that
had kept her awake. A child with a large backpack got on, sat across the aisle,
and unwrapped a sandwich. The bus turned toward the water, and the light changed
from gray to pale gold as the day arrived. She felt calmer, not because the
problem was gone, but because movement beat lying in the dark, inventing new
fears. At her stop she thanked the driver and walked the last two blocks in air
that still stung, but in a way she had learned to like.

He opened the ticket and read it twice. The first line was the title he expected.
The second line was a new name, a partner he had not been told about. The third
line was a short note: please confirm by five. He walked to the window, looked
down at the empty street, and then returned to the screen. The honest reply was
one sentence long, but he drafted three versions first, because tone mattered
more than length. When he sent the message, the timestamp showed one minute under
the deadline, which he would later find funny, though he did not smile yet.

=== GOOD: CAUSE AND EFFECT, CAREFUL HEDGING ===
Because the sample was small, the estimate is noisy, and we do not know whether
a larger study would find the same direction of effect. If the weather closes the
airport, we will rebook, but we have not been notified yet. The bug appears when
the cache is full, and then the client sends duplicate requests, which the server
rejects, so the user sees an error. Until we add a back-pressure signal, the
workaround is to clear the local cache and restart, which is annoying but safe.

The city grew because trade routes converged at the river, and later because a
new rail line reduced the cost of moving grain. A historian might argue for one
force over another, but a simple story that ignores the river would miss the
physical basis for the first settlement. Maps from two centuries ago show a
smaller town with the same pattern of streets, which is evidence, not a guarantee,
about the persistence of a plan.

=== GOOD: INSTRUCTIONS IN ORDER ===
To reset the device, first unplug the power. Wait ten seconds. Press the small
recessed button on the back with a paperclip for five seconds. While you hold the
button, have someone else plug the power in again. When the light blinks, release
the button. The device will boot into recovery mode, where the screen shows a
list of options. This sequence is an example; follow the manual for your device.

To summarize an article, read the abstract and the first and last sections.
Then read the middle with a light pencil mark next to a claim in each paragraph.
When you write your summary, list only the claims you marked, and add no new
criticism that the author did not invite. A summary is not a review unless you
label it as such.

=== GOOD: DIALOG, STANDARD PUNCTUATION ===
"I do not know yet," she said. "I will know after the call."
He asked, "When does the call start?"
"At three. Can you be there?"
"Yes, but I will need the dial-in number."
"Of course. I will forward the invite now."

The rules are simple. Use a comma before a tag like "she said" if the tag follows
a full clause. Question marks and exclamation points stay with the question or
exclamation, not doubled at the end of a tag. Each new speaker begins a new
paragraph in formal narrative.

=== SENTENCE VARIETY: RHYTHM ===
Long sentences have their place when the clauses are parallel and the reader can
follow, but a page of only long sentences is tiring. Short sentences help. They
punch. A mix of lengths is natural in clear prose. A draft that has ten sentences
in a row with the same opening pattern, such as "The X is" or "It is," can often
be revised by moving one adverb, combining two thoughts, or breaking one long line
in half for emphasis.

=== VOCABULARY: PRECISE BUT PLAIN ===
A book can be long or lengthy; "lengthy" sometimes adds a sense of tedium, so
choose on purpose, not at random. "Fewer" goes with count nouns, as in fewer
errors; "less" goes with mass and degree, as in less noise. Affect and effect
are a common pair: the verb is often "affect," the noun, "effect," with exceptions
in formal psychology, where a noun "affect" can appear, rare in general prose.
"Between" is for two clear items; "among" fits many, though "between" appears in
a phrase like "between the four of us" when the group is small and named. These
nuances are training signal for a model that will write, not a license to pedant
in chat.

=== MORE WELL-FORMED DECLARATIVES (FOR TOKEN STATISTICS) ===
The report is due on Friday. The data are in the folder, if you use British
plural, or: The data is in the folder, in many modern software contexts. The
phenomenon is rare, but the phenomena in this set are not all rare, so the writer
chose a simpler phrasing. The analysis suggests a small benefit. The analysis does
not prove causation. The results align with the earlier work in three of four
comparisons. The fourth comparison has a wide interval, so we do not overstate the
agreement.

We measured height in centimeters, weight in kilograms, and blood pressure in
millimeters of mercury. The units are consistent within each column, and a note
in the table caption names the day of calibration for the scale. A reader who
works in other units can convert, and the paper lists the constant factors. Clarity
helps replication, which is the point of a methods section.

The meeting had three goals, and the chair stated them in the first minute. The
first goal was to agree on a timeline. The second was to assign owners. The third
was to set a process for a mid-week check that would not add a new standing
meeting unless the data showed a need. Everyone left with the same list in a
shared document, which is better than a memory of a summary.

=== WELL-FORMED QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS (CLEAN REGISTER) ===
Q: How do I know when to use "who" and when to use "whom"? A: "Who" acts as a
subject, as in, Who left the file open? "Whom" is an object, as in, To whom should
I send the form, or, Whom did you ask? In informal speech, many people say "Who
did you ask," but edited prose often keeps "whom" after a preposition for clarity
and register.
Q: Is it wrong to end a sentence with a preposition? A: In modern English, not
usually; clarity beats a contorted phrase. What are you working on, is natural.
Q: Is it wrong to split an infinitive? A: Not a crime; "to go boldly" and "to
boldly go" are both used; choose the rhythm the sentence needs, not a fake rule
from a different century.
Q: What is the Oxford comma? A: A comma before the last item in a list, which can
remove ambiguity, as in, We thank our parents, A, and B, if A and B are the names
of two people, not "parents" plus "A and B" smushed. Style guides differ; the key
is consistency within a document and clarity for the reader.
Q: What is a dangling modifier? A: A description that is not next to the noun it
is meant to describe, as in, Walking to the bus, the rain started, which reads as
if the rain walked, unless you fix it. Better: As I walked to the bus, it started
raining, or: Walking to the bus, I felt the first drops.

=== AVOID / CORRECT PAIRS (LABELS ARE PART OF THE TRAINING) ===
Avoid, vague: "It is a question of things and stuff." Better: "The issue is the
size of the budget, not the schedule, so we can decide today."
Avoid, double negative with confusion: "I do not have no time." In standard English:
"I have no time," or, "I do not have time," not two negatives in one, unless
dialect is a deliberate style choice in fiction.
Avoid, subject missing: "After checking the file, the bug was not seen." The
file did not see the bug; a person should be the one checking. Better: "After
checking the file, the engineer did not see the bug," or: "After the file was
checked, the bug was not found."
Correct: The engineer checked the file, but the bug was not there. The engineer
looked in the right place, but the wrong build was on the test machine, which is a
different class of error.

=== PASSAGES FOR COHERENCE: MILD OPINION, SUPPORTED ===
A city needs reliable transit, safe water, and schools that serve every child, not
only a subset. Money alone does not build trust; process does. When a project is
rushed without public comment, the next project faces resistance even if the plan
is sound, because people need to feel that the rules are fair. A long review can
seem slow, so the balance is a realistic schedule, open documents, and a way to
answer questions in plain language. This is not a manifesto, only a paragraph a
civic reader can follow without guessing what the next sentence will do.

A small team can move quickly when the scope is narrow, the spec is real, and the
decision makers sit in the same room at least once a week. When the product grows
past that size, the old habits break unless someone rewrites the org chart, not
the code, and the rewrite has to be honest. Adding five people to a three-person
meeting is not a larger meeting in the same sense; it is a new meeting with a
new set of side conversations. Clear writing about roles helps more than a
poster about agility.

=== PASSAGES: DEFINITION, THEN EXAMPLE, THEN TAKEAWAY ===
A checksum is a short number or string that summarizes a file for error detection.
If a single bit flips, the checksum usually changes, and the system knows the copy
is bad. A checksum is not a secret: anyone can recompute it if they have the
algorithm and the file, so a checksum is not, by itself, a security system; it
detects honest mistakes, not a clever attacker, unless you combine it with
signing. The takeaway: use it in downloads and in backups, and do not call it
encryption. That three-part shape, definition, example, takeaway, is good training
for text that "makes sense" as a unit.

A histogram shows how often values fall in bins, not a line through time, unless
the x-axis is an ordered time bin. If you have ten thousand test scores, a
histogram of scores answers "how many people scored near each value," and you
see modes and skew. A box plot, by contrast, marks quartiles. Each chart is right
for a question, not for every question. A paper that slaps a chart in without
stating the question is weak; a good caption states the data source, the
variable, the units, and the sample size, all in a few lines. That is another
coherent block for the model to learn.

=== RANDOM COHERENT FACTS IN CLEAR SENTENCES (SINGLE DOMAIN PER BLOCK) ===
Mars is smaller than Earth, with a thin atmosphere, mostly carbon dioxide, and
surface features that show ancient water flows in some areas. The day on Mars is
a little longer than a day on Earth, which matters for landing windows and
communication schedules, not for poetry. Rovers on Mars send data through relay
orbits, because a direct line to the deep-space network is not always available.
A paragraph like this is simple, with one topic and no fake drama.

A river delta forms where a river meets standing water, slows, and drops sediment.
The shape of the land changes over centuries; maps need updates. Deltas are often
fertile for agriculture, and also at risk when sea level rises or when upstream
dams trap sediment that the delta used to receive. Clear sentences, one process
at a time, are good grammar for a reader who does not work in the field, but
wants to read without stopping every line.

=== WELL-MADE LISTS, PARALLEL, WITH INTRO SENTENCE ===
The handoff will succeed if the following are true. First, the build is in the
right branch, tagged, and not ahead of a merge that failed. Second, the release
notes list user-visible changes, and nothing else, because internal refactors
belong in the team log, not the customer page. Third, a named person is on call for
eight hours after the push, and that person is not the same person who wrote every
line of the release, to reduce blind spots. A list of three, each item parallel in
form, is easier to read than a run of sentences that hide the number of
requirements. That style is a skill worth modeling.

The recipe needs four ingredients, all in one bowl before the oven. First, measure
the flour and the salt into a dry bowl, and stir once. Second, crack the eggs into
a small cup, check for shell, and add the eggs. Third, melt the butter, cool it
until it is warm, not hot, and pour the butter. Fourth, fold the wet and dry
until no dry streaks remain, then stop, because more folding makes the result
tough. Sentences that begin with a word like "first" set an expectation, so the
reader is satisfied when the pattern continues.

This file rewards the model for predicting standard English, complete clauses,
punctuation that matches the pace of speech, and short paragraphs with one main
point each. Bigger or older corpora are still the backbone of a training run; this
layer is a grammar-forward supplement. Retrain, do not just append, if you need
the weights to move.
